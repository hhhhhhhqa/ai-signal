These issues take on a particularly tangible dimension in conflict situations. In the Democratic Republic of the Congo, as in other theaters of crisis, surveillance technologies and drones already play a growing role in the operational environment. These technologies can contribute to reconnaissance, observation, and ceasefire monitoring. They can also be diverted for military reconnaissance, coordination, disinformation, or propaganda purposes. This ambivalis reminds us of an essential truth. No technology is in itself an instrument of peace or conflict. It is the framework within which it is designed, deployed and governed that largely determines its impact. That is why responsibility must remain at the heart of our reflection. States have a responsibility to establish appropriate legal frameworks, ensure effective oversight, and prevent risks that could affect international peace and security. The scientific community and civil society must likewise be able to contribute their independent expertise and participate in the critical assessment of risks. It requires closer international cooperation based in particular on the exchange of information concerning serious incidents and identified vulnerabilities, cooperation and evaluation and safety testing, as well as the establishment of communication channels among governness, government scientists, and technology stakeholders. President, for the Democratic Republic of the Congo, this cooperation must also meet a fundamental requirement, that of inclusion. Developing countries cannot be reduced to the role of mere users of technologies designed elsewhere, nor should be the mere recipients of standards and norms that they did not adequately participate in shaping. They must possess the scientific, technical and institutional capacities that enable them to assess the technologies to which their populations and economies are exposed to participate in their governance and above all to contribute to innovation themselves. This requirement of inclusion leads us to consider artificial intelligence across its entire value chain. For despite its seemingly immaterial nature, the AI revolution rests upon profoundly material realities, infrastructure, energy, industrial capabilities, and strategic natural resources. This dimension is of particular importance to the Democratic Republic of the Congo. During its presidency of the security council last July, my country invited the council to reflect on how responsible, transparent and equitable governance of natural resources can contribute to conflict prevention, supply chain security and the emergence of
d prosperity. The AI revolution gives this reflection additional relevance. We must ensure that technological transformations of the 21st century do not reproduce the economic imbalances of previous decades. Countries supplying essential resources to the global economy while processing technology and most of the added value remain concentrated elsewhere. The security of supply chains cannot be separated from the prosperity of the populations at their source. That is why AI governance must also be approached from a logic of
d responsibility. Responsibility in the design of systems, responsibility in their use and responsibility within the value chains that make their development possible and also responsibility in the distribution of the benefits of technological progress. For the Democratic Republic of the Congo, the objective is clear. The resources that help make the digital revolution possible must also contribute to the development, economic transformation, industrialization, and strengthening of the technological capabilities of those countries that produce them. This is not merely a matter of development. It is also a matter of peace. When value chains are opaque and benefits are distributed unequally and when producing communities remain excluded from the prosperity generated by their own resources, economic vulnerabilities can fuel tensions and conflict economies. Conversely, when resources are governed responsibly, when local processing creates jobs and opportunities, and when populations genuinely
in the benefits of growth, supply chains can not only become more secure and resilient, but also chains of
d prosperity. Mr. President, it is precisely here that the Security Council can bring added value. It is not the council's role to replace the appropriate bodies responsible for developing the full range of technical rules relating to AI, but it is fully within the council's remmit to anticipate the risks that may affect international peace and security, to consider how new technologies transform conflict dynamics, to protect civilians, to encourage confidence building measures, and to ensure that technological transformations do not create new vulnerabilities that could fuel and stability. Our objective must therefore not be to choose between innovation and security any more than it should be to choose between natural resources and development. Our responsibility is to govern progress so that it serves humanity.
